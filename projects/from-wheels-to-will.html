Sheet 04 · Dissertation

From Wheels to Will

Planning for behavioural change in Auckland transport — my honours dissertation, on why new infrastructure alone has not moved Aucklanders out of their cars, and how psychology could be built into transport policy.

Date
November 2025
Context
BUrbPlan (Hons) dissertation, University of Auckland
Role
Individual research
Methods
Bibliometric review, survey re-analysis, policy mapping, case comparison
Cases
Stockholm, Copenhagen, Singapore
Location
Auckland

In shortA four-stage response

Lasting change comes when physical interventions are paired with strategies that shift perception, reinforce experience and reshape habits.

  1. Experience

    Low-risk chances to try alternatives: free or capped fare periods, reversible congestion-charging trials with immediate public transport upgrades.

  2. Positive reinforcement

    Make first trips consistently good: safe, reliable, continuous infrastructure such as protected cycle corridors.

  3. Attitude shift

    Make the benefits visible and socially credible, as Copenhagen did for cycling, and add a public decision step to build legitimacy.

  4. Habit formation

    Sustain the improved network so that new routines outlast the trial — price signals alone tend to fade.

01Question and approach

How can psychological factors be integrated into behaviour-change policy to support sustainable transport mode shift in Auckland?

  • A bibliometric review of research on mode choice and psychology from Web of Science, mapped as a keyword network in VOSviewer.
  • A re-analysis of Auckland Council’s survey of 2,799 drivers, re-coded into psychological barriers.
  • A review of Auckland and New Zealand transport policies against those barriers.
  • International cases: Stockholm’s congestion charge, Copenhagen’s cycling strategy and Singapore’s road pricing.

02Four barriers that lock in car use

The four mechanisms reinforce one another, so policies that act on only one of them tend to underperform.

Habit

Automatic, cue-based driving routines bypass conscious choice.

Social norms

“Motonormativity” treats driving as the normal, expected default.

Attitudes

Cars are seen as fast, comfortable and autonomous; public transport as unreliable and inconvenient.

Perceived control

Low confidence in using buses, trains or bikes, heightened by safety concerns and poor information.

  1. Contextual cues
  2. Automatic response: drive the usual way
  3. Less deliberation
  4. Fewer trips by bus, train or bike
  5. Attitudes never updated
  6. ↺ back to the same cues

03What policy misses

Auckland’s transport strategies make real progress on simplicity and safety — fare caps and pedestrian safety upgrades — but rarely tackle habit or social norms. Behavioural goals appear in strategy documents, yet they are seldom delivered through structured, sequenced interventions that link infrastructure, incentives and communication.

Infrastructure creates the opportunity for change; psychology decides whether it is taken up, sustained and normalised.
